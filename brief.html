Geopolitics

Middle East tension, oil, and trade politics

Oil markets stayed on edge this week after attacks on Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline and continued regional hostilities kept a floor under crude prices. The renewed rise in oil prices, which pushed crude back above $100 a barrel, continues to cloud the inflation outlook, particularly as tensions in the Middle East remain elevated and risks to oil supplies increase.

Houthi forces have added a new wrinkle to the shipping-lane story this week. Iran-backed Houthi militants are advancing towards the Bab el-Mandeb Strait while intensifying attacks on Saudi targets and regional shipping routes. By Thursday, oil had eased slightly as Riyadh worked around the outage. Brent futures, the international benchmark, was slightly lower at $105.81 per barrel, while U.S. crude oil was down 0.22% at $102.14 a barrel, as Saudi Arabia made additional crude cargoes available to Asian refiners through ship-to-ship transfers.

Separately, Washington is moving on energy sanctions with trade implications. The House has cleared a procedural path toward a final vote on sweeping Russia and Iran sanctions legislation that would give President Donald Trump authority to impose tariffs of up to 100% on major buyers of Russian energy. That's a lever that could ripple into energy and shipping-linked equities over the coming weeks.

The Fed and rates

First hike since 2023, yields near 2007 highs

The headline event of the week: the Fed moved off the sidelines. In a unanimous decision, the Fed raised the overnight funds rate by a quarter percentage point, bringing the target range to between 3.75% and 4%. Chair Warsh's tone afterward mattered as much as the move itself. Treasuries pared losses and US equity-index futures climbed as Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh's resolve to tackle inflation reassured markets following the central bank's first interest-rate hike since 2023.

The bond market's reaction has been the bigger story for options traders. The 10-year Treasury note yield moved above the key 5% mark after the rate increase, with the benchmark yield up 2 basis points at 5.016%. Bloomberg's Thursday coverage noted a partial reversal: yields on the benchmark 10-year and the 30-year bonds both dropped by around two basis points.

Where rates go next is genuinely contested across pricing venues. The CME FedWatch Tool put the odds of another 25-basis-point hike at the next FOMC meeting, on October 28, 2026, at 55.1% as of September 17. Strategists have also trimmed their outlooks: the president of Yardeni Research slashed his year-end S&P 500 target to 7,900 from 8,400, a new forecast signaling upside of 4.1% from Tuesday's close of 7,585.73, a far cry from the 11% gain implied by the old estimate.

AI and tech

A pause in the trade, not an exit

Big Tech has quietly had a rough year relative to the broader index, and rising yields aren't helping. The swath of stocks has underperformed the S&P 500 for 92% of the year to date, Barclays' Stefano Pascale said, with the firm's analysis suggesting multiple compression has been the primary driver of Big Tech's underperformance this year. That's a valuation story more than a fundamentals one.

Underneath the megacaps, the infrastructure side of the AI trade still looks busy. Micron posted fiscal Q3 2026 revenue up 345.7% year over year, while Broadcom reported fiscal Q3 revenue of $29.59 billion, up 85.5% year over year. But there's a supply-timing wrinkle worth watching: analysts from JPMorgan estimate that approximately 60% of data center capacity planned for completion in 2027 hasn't even broken ground yet.

The political dimension is increasingly about power, not chips. In some localities, data centers have become a flashpoint in debates over power availability and electricity costs, and for AI data centers, securing reliable power access seems now more important than construction readiness. Trade flows are shifting too: US AI-related imports nearly doubled to $260 billion, while China's intermediate-goods exports rose 27% and represented almost three-quarters of its export growth.

Technology weakness led to a portfolio decline lagging the S&P 500, while Energy showed strong momentum, moving into the leading quadrant; Health Care and Financials remained stable despite losing some relative momentum, and Materials are at risk, breaking below long-term support lines. Separately, a broader sector dashboard flagged the same defensive-versus-cyclical split: Utilities and Industrials continued to lag the broader market.

At the single-stock level, Wednesday's Fed reaction hit financials and industrials hardest. Leading the losses are IBM (-4.32%), Goldman Sachs (-3.92%) and Boeing (-3.69%). Smaller, more rate-sensitive names have also shown wider swings than the S&P during recent risk-off weeks tied to yield moves.
