Geopolitics

Iran war still unresolved

Traders were told to watch the Middle East again this morning, as futures desks flagged ongoing developments alongside the jobs report. U.S. stock futures are trending slightly higher early Friday as investors brace for the critical August jobs report and monitor geopolitical developments in the Middle East.

The human and market cost showed up in the same headline: tensions continue to rise following an explosion at a wedding in southern Iran that killed four people and injured at least 68 others, according to Reuters. Oil still finished the week lower even so, with Bloomberg noting oil retreated at the end of a volatile week.

Morgan Stanley's house view is that this conflict shouldn't be treated as a one-off shock. Its policy strategist added a blunt caveat worth sitting with — "Markets may tolerate uncertainty for now, but prolonged uncertainty will be harder to look through," said Monica Guerra, Head of US Policy, Morgan Stanley Wealth Management.

The Fed and rates

Jobs beat flips the hike odds

The headline number did the damage: the U.S. added 162,000 jobs in August, decisively beating consensus estimates, and CNBC put the miss-versus-beat in context, reporting nonfarm payrolls grew 162,000 last month, much more than the 53,000 that had been expected. Bloomberg framed the market reaction directly — a stronger-than-anticipated increase in US jobs drove stocks and short-dated bonds lower, with traders boosting their bets the Federal Reserve will raise rates this month.

This wasn't a straight line. A day earlier, Fed Governor Waller's more balanced tone had pushed market pricing for a rate hike this month back to roughly 50%, undoing some of the hawkish momentum built by Fed Chair Warsh's Jackson Hole remarks, where the odds of a September hike had already climbed to somewhere between 60 and 67%, largely on the back of Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole speech in late August. After Friday's jobs data, traders increased the probability of a September Fed rate hike to roughly 58%, according to CME Group's FedWatch Tool.

There's also a political layer to this: the Federal Reserve should cut interest rates to make homes more affordable, Vice President JD Vance said Thursday, adding to the pressure President Donald Trump has placed on the central bank to cut borrowing costs. That pressure is running in the opposite direction from what today's labor data implies for policy.

AI and tech

Capex boom meets local backlash

The spending story hasn't slowed down. Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta are leading the spending spree, with their combined 2026 capital expenditure expected to reach roughly $745 billion this year, much of it directed toward AI infrastructure and data centers. The Trump administration has been vocal that this buildout should continue largely unimpeded, warning towns that block new data centers risk being left behind economically.

That message runs into real friction. Gallup found 71% of Americans oppose local data centers, a level of opposition surpassing peak anti-nuclear sentiment, while opposition groups blocked $130 billion in Q1 2026 alone. Even inside the president's own party, Abbott's moratorium on new grid connections halted up to 1,800 Texas projects, putting him at public odds with Trump over AI infrastructure.

The theme has also proven volatile for investors positioned in it. Earlier this year, maximum drawdowns for AI stocks like Micron and Intel reaching 40% or even higher at one point showed how sharply sentiment can swing even inside a structurally growing theme, something today's memory-chip gains-amid-a-down-tape are a small reminder of.

Market internals

Defensive tilt, one earnings casualty

Today's leadership was narrow and defensive. Utilities is the only sector in the green, while consumer cyclicals and communication services saw the biggest declines. That's a fairly classic "rates just moved against risk" pattern, consistent with the jobs-driven repricing of Fed odds described above.

The single biggest single-stock move was Lululemon, where Lululemon Athletica tumbled 18% on news of declining revenue and a cut to its outlook. Elsewhere, Fair Isaac (FICO -15.93%) plummeted following pricing criticism on social media from the Federal Housing Finance Agency, while on the other side of the ledger memory stocks, such as Sandisk and Micron Technology, gained despite broader stock market losses.

Smaller-cap names held up better than the headline indexes today, with the small-cap Russell 2000 index outperformed the broader market on Friday, rising 0.1% in afternoon trading. Underneath the jobs number itself, CNBC's analysis found women accounted for 158,000 — or around 98% — of the 162,000 jobs added in the month, a detail that's more of a labor-market story than a trading one, but shows how uneven the "beat" actually was under the surface.
