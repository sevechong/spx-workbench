Geopolitics

Middle East energy shock

The dominant driver overnight was energy-market disruption tied to the ongoing conflict in the Middle East. Oil prices surged after Yemen's Iran-aligned Houthi forces launched several attacks against Saudi Arabia and captured the strategic island of Perim in the Bab al-Mandab Strait on Sunday, The Guardian reported, and separately Saudi Arabia shut down a critical oil pipeline after accusing militants in Iraq of launching drone attacks on the export route, which is meant to bypass a partly closed Strait of Hormuz.

The scale of the move has been building for over a week: Brent gained nearly 9% last week as attacks on energy infrastructure and disruptions around key shipping routes raised fears of prolonged supply shortages. Analysts have described oil as the market's central geopolitical story right now rather than a side issue.

Beyond the Gulf, September's broader calendar is politically loaded. The month brings key elections, the Xi-Trump summit, more of the US-Iran war and major UN, SCO and BRICS+ summits, any of which could add fresh headline risk on short notice.

The Fed and rates

Hike odds, not cut odds

Unlike prior cycles this year, the debate now is whether the Fed hikes rather than cuts. Markets are currently pricing in an 86% probability that the Fed will raise its policy rate by 25 basis points on Wednesday, with another rate hike expected later this year, a shift traced back to hotter inflation prints and stickier energy costs.

At the prior meeting in July, the Fed policy committee voted 9-3 to keep rates at 3.5%-3.75% in the face of inflationary pressures, with Chair Kevin Warsh describing internal debate as a "good family fight" while stressing unity on price stability. Those dissents were widely read as building pressure toward a September move.

Bond markets are reflecting that same anxiety. The 10-year Treasury yield is closing in on 5%, a level last touched in October 2023, and the benchmark yield is hovering around 4.96%. Strategists quoted by CNBC noted that a growth-driven rise could be manageable, while inflation, fiscal concerns or market stress pose greater risks if the 5% line is crossed.

AI and tech

Safety warnings meet high valuations

Tech is the other major swing factor this morning. US stock futures sank on Monday morning after the two leading frontier artificial intelligence companies called for an industrywide slowdown of AI development, sending tech stocks tumbling, with Nasdaq-100 futures falling 1.8%, led by losses in chip stocks including Nvidia. This adds a political and safety-policy dimension to what had been a purely earnings-driven AI trade.

Separately, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman said in an interview published on Saturday that the AI startup would not go public this year, removing a widely anticipated listing from near-term investor calculations. Memory-chip suppliers remain a bright spot within the theme: DRAM pricing has soared because AI data centers are gobbling up a huge portion of available production, which manifests downstream as tighter supply and better pricing for component makers like Micron, whose results land after the closing bell on Sept. 30.

Market internals

Energy up, small caps left behind

Leadership has rotated hard toward hard assets this year. Eight of the 11 S&P sectors are higher so far in 2026, with energy leading, up 43%, while consumer discretionary is lagging, down 2.3%. Friday's session showed a similar split at the stock level, with gains led by Cisco Systems, IBM and Boeing, while the biggest losers were UnitedHealth, Amgen and Merck.

Breadth by company size has also been uneven. In the prior holiday-shortened week, the Russell 2000 and S&P MidCap 400 indexes fared worst, dropping 2.41% and 1.87% respectively, while the Nasdaq Composite held up best with a 0.66% decline, a pattern consistent with investors sheltering in large, liquid names during the oil-driven volatility.
