Geopolitics

Iran war spills into oil markets

U.S. and Iranian forces have been trading strikes for months, and this week the fighting shifted more directly onto tankers and energy infrastructure. Washington and Tehran escalated tit-for-tat attacks, with the U.S. targeting Iranian oil tankers and Iran firing on U.S. warships patrolling near the Strait of Hormuz, which traders read as a genuine escalation rather than posturing.

Regional spillover is now part of the story too: equity markets opened mostly lower this week following reports of overnight attacks on Saudi energy facilities, with WTI crude trading around $93 a barrel. Separately, Brent crude was reported surging toward $100 per barrel amid the escalating U.S.-Iran military exchanges.

Trade friction adds another layer: retaliatory tariffs from Canada on about $20 billion of U.S. goods took effect this week, after the president said a Canadian aircraft maker can't sell in the U.S. unless production moves onshore. Separately, a British luxury automaker said it will cut 4,000 jobs, citing tariffs among the pressures.

The Fed and rates

Hike odds near a coin flip

The Fed's own chair has spent the last two weeks talking markets toward a hike rather than a cut. Before his Jackson Hole speech, investors put the odds of a September rate hike at about one in three; after it, that likelihood rose above 50/50, and fed funds futures have since shown roughly a 60.4% chance of a quarter-point hike, according to the CME's FedWatch tool.

Not every policymaker agrees: bonds sold off after a pledge from the Fed chair to tame inflation, before recovering after a Fed governor said he would support keeping rates unchanged if inflation keeps moving toward target. That split is playing out publicly, since the Vice President said the Fed should cut rates to make homes more affordable, just days after the Fed chair hinted at the opposite.

Bond markets are feeling the tension: a sustained break above 4.8% on the 10-year could create "meaningful problems" across other asset classes, one strategist warned, while HSBC raised its year-end 10-year yield forecast to 4.65%, citing a more hawkish distribution of possible Fed outcomes.

AI and tech

Spending up, scrutiny up too

The capital flowing into AI infrastructure keeps growing even as questions about payback periods multiply. One estimate points to roughly $2 trillion of global AI spending in 2026, and the "Magnificent Seven" alone are expected to deploy $527 billion in AI and data-center capital expenditures this fiscal year, up $62 billion from prior estimates.

Supply bottlenecks are a live concern: JPMorgan analysts estimate roughly 60% of data-center capacity planned for completion in 2027 hasn't even broken ground yet, which is part of why AI-linked stocks have been described as being whipsawed this year even as chip names remain a top performer.

The political dimension is explicit at the Fed itself: the massive investment in AI data centers is contributing to higher inflation by driving up construction and memory-chip costs, even as the Fed chair has called AI a "hinge point in history" and said it will not shape near-term rate decisions.

Sector leadership has stayed remarkably stable this year. Eight of the eleven S&P sectors are higher so far in 2026, with energy leading, up 43%, while consumer discretionary is lagging, down 2.3%.
